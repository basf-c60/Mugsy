Mugsy: annotated disassembly

Overview

Mugsy (Melbourne House, 1984) is a "comic strip" strategy game. You are Mugsy, boss of a 1920s protection racket, and your sidekick Louey reports to you each year in speech bubbles over full-screen pictures. You decide how many clients to buy or sell, how much to spend on artillery and ammo, how many clients to squeeze, how much to hand to the cops as a kickback, and whether to pay off rival mobs or your own disgruntled hoods. The game is meant to last 7 to 11 years (chosen at random, but see the bug about the game rarely ending on time) unless you are rubbed out first, and ends with a percentage score and a rating.
Loading. The tape holds a BASIC loader (CLEAR 24575, then LOAD "s" CODE 16384 for the loading screen and LOAD "mugsy" CODE for 40,960 bytes at 24576-65535) and starts the game with PRINT USR 24576 (24576). There is no copy protection; the TAP and TZX images in this project have identical code and screen blocks.
Economics. The yearly model, what each of your answers really does and the best strategy are on the economics page. In short: squeeze everybody, buy clients, spend about 1.5 grand per hood on ammo, pay a large (but not total) kickback, keep nothing in the safe, and pay a small handout (about 0.1 grand per hood) that gives Mugsy the full 8 hit points for the shootout.
Structure. Almost all of the game logic is written not in Z80 code but in a compact bytecode, interpreted by the routine at 30092 (see the VM reference). The script for one year is the block at 24652. The VM has 84 signed 32-bit fixed-point variables (two decimal places; see the variables page), nine flag bytes at 33888, and op 14 calls native Z80 routines for input (27790, 27753), the hush-money scene (28297), the end-of-game rating (26740), the "A year goes by" animation (33994) and the hit-man shootout (28358).
Text. Louey's lines (49774) are compressed. The 32 commonest words are single bytes (96-127, via 49710) and the other words are two-byte references into a dictionary of 225 words (48640). Control codes insert numbers and money values taken from the VM stream after the SAY instruction, pick random alternatives, or repeat earlier text (29458). The rendered text is word-wrapped into an oval speech bubble (28757) in a six-pixel-high proportional font (27087), while the status lines use the ROM font (27724).
Pictures. The bubble backgrounds are vector pictures drawn by 31247 from command lists (lines, flood fills, bitmap blocks and attribute runs), chosen at random from two tables of layouts (33909). The two full-screen pictures used in the animations (the Partridge Club and the street) are in a more compact bit-stream format drawn by 32126. All nine pictures are shown on the pictures page. The picture engine is a descendant of the one in The Hobbit; see the Hobbit page.
Animations and arcade sequence. At the end of each year one of two IM2-driven animations plays (33994): the hoods passing through the Partridge Club, or the getaway car. Nothing in the game decides which: it is a random choice, and because the random routine is uneven the club comes up about 60% of the time and the car about 40% (see the trivia page). If your hoods put out a contract on you, the hit man from Detroit arrives and you play an arcade shootout in a restaurant (42112), controlled with Q/Z (up/down), I/P (left/right) and B/N/M (fire).

How this disassembly was made. The code was separated from the data by running the game in SkoolKit's Z80 simulator with scripted keypresses for many games (recording every executed address), then following every branch statically from those addresses. The VM scripts were traced from the nine places that start the interpreter, and the messages were decoded with a Python reimplementation of the text renderer. About 95% of the code instructions have comments written by hand (the shootout, the animations and the picture routines were also checked by running them in the simulator); the rest carry comments generated from a table of known variables and routines, so they say what the instruction does in game terms where that is known and literally otherwise. The generated mugsy.asm also assembles, with an unmodified copy of sjasmplus 1.24.1 (sjasmplus --raw=out.bin mugsy.asm), to exactly the same 40,960 bytes as the tape, in addition to SkoolKit's own skool2bin check.
